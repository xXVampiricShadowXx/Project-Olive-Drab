# Prototype Roleplay Layer

This guide adds a lightweight roleplay layer to the first playable Olive Drab
prototype. It gives each commander enough identity and motivation to make
decisions feel consequential without adding a character-build system, private
mechanical powers, or extra bookkeeping.

The prototype has two player commanders, one for the NATO company and one for
the Russian company, plus one neutral GM. The setting is fictionalized. Roleplay
should support the command decisions already defined by the scenario and rules,
not turn the session into a historical reenactment or a debate about real-world
politics.

## What this layer is for

Roleplay should help players answer:

- What does this commander notice, value, and fear?
- How do they explain uncertainty to subordinates and superiors?
- Which risks are they willing to accept to meet the objective?
- What kind of relationship do they want with the other commander?

Roleplay may shape the wording, priority, timing, and risk tolerance of an
order. It may create useful questions, requests, friction, or opportunities for
the GM to frame a report. It does not change unit statistics, reveal hidden
information, skip an action timer, alter a resolution roll, or grant an
unlisted ability.

## Minimum commander creation

Complete this in five minutes before the first active window. Each commander
writes one or two sentences for each prompt and shares the result with the GM.
The opposing commander receives only what is made public in play.

```text
Name and call sign:
Current command role:
Identity and background:
Command style:
Primary motivation:
Scenario objective:
One strength:
One complication:
Important relationship:
What this commander will not risk without a reason:
```

Keep the answers concrete and playable. A background is a perspective, not a
new source of expertise. A strength is a tendency the player can portray, not a
bonus. A complication is a source of pressure or difficult choices, not a
punishment imposed without the player's agreement.

### Identity and background

Choose only details that can matter during this scenario: prior unit
experience, civilian or military education, local familiarity, a professional
reputation, language or communication habits, or a recent event that affects
judgment. Do not use background to establish facts about the map, enemy, or
scenario that the GM has not provided.

### Command style

Select a default style and describe how it appears in orders and reports:
deliberate, aggressive, protective, improvisational, procedural, diplomatic, or
another concise description. The style is a roleplay prompt. It never changes
the action procedure or permits an order outside the commander's authority.

### Motivations and objectives

The published scenario objective remains the win condition. Add one primary
personal motivation and, if useful, one secondary motivation that can pull
against it. Examples include preserving the company, proving reliability,
protecting a route, avoiding unnecessary escalation, or earning trust.

Motivations can explain why a commander chooses one legal option over another.
They cannot replace the objective, create a new victory condition, or force
another player to cooperate.

### Strengths and complications

Choose one strength and one complication that are visible in decisions and
relationships. Examples:

- Strength: calm under incomplete reports; complication: reluctance to commit.
- Strength: inspires confidence; complication: overpromises certainty.
- Strength: plans contingencies; complication: slow to accept an unplanned
  opportunity.
- Strength: protects subordinates; complication: resists a costly objective.

The GM may reflect these traits in fiction, questions, and the consequences
already available in the rules. The GM must not convert them into hidden
modifiers or automatic success unless a future rules revision explicitly adds
that mechanic for everyone.

## Relationships and chain-of-command roleplay

The commander role includes relationships with superiors, subordinates, staff,
and the opposing commander even when those people are represented by brief
messages rather than separate players.

Use the established role-based communication chain. A commander can:

- Explain intent and constraints to a superior.
- Give clear priorities, limits, and fallback behavior to subordinates.
- Ask for clarification, resources, reports, or permission.
- Build trust or create friction through consistent communication.
- Request GM-framed NPC or opposing-side contact when the procedure allows it.

Relationships should affect the fictional dialogue, framing, and perceived
credibility of interactions; they do not alter published response windows,
action timers, or other formal procedures. The GM should keep NPC reactions
proportionate to the established situation and should offer the commander a
meaningful choice rather than dictating a personality.

When attention is intermittent, write relationship intent into the order:

```text
Intent:
What I need the subordinate or superior to understand:
What they may decide without me:
What requires confirmation:
Fallback if I cannot be reached:
```

This keeps roleplay usable during short visits to the active window and makes
absence handling consistent with the operating procedure.

## NPC guidance for the GM

NPCs are a framing tool for reports, requests, and command pressure. In this
prototype, keep them lightweight:

1. Give an NPC a role, a visible concern, and one practical request.
2. Tie the request to information the NPC could reasonably possess.
3. Offer a response deadline or a clear consequence of delay when appropriate.
4. Let the commander answer, delegate, refuse, or ask for more information.
5. Record any material decision in the authoritative log.

NPCs may represent a superior, subordinate, liaison, local official, medical
lead, or other approved contact. They may not introduce an unannounced faction,
secret objective, special equipment, or mechanical exception. Do not use an
NPC to correct a player's choice, deliver information the side has not earned,
or force a dramatic scene when the player is trying to resolve an urgent
command decision.

If an NPC interaction would create a new rule, pause the interaction long
enough to state a temporary ruling, apply it symmetrically where applicable,
and record it for post-game review.

## Information and roleplay boundaries

Stay inside the commander's information layer. A player may portray a belief,
rumor, suspicion, or bluff, but should label it when submitting a report or
order. The GM continues to distinguish confirmed, reported, and suspected
information.

Roleplay must not:

- Turn player knowledge, rules text, or out-of-character discussion into
  in-character intelligence.
- Reveal the master map, hidden starting information, or unrevealed modifiers.
- Treat confident delivery as confirmation.
- Change an order after learning its result without recording a new order.
- Use personal chat or outside research as an in-game channel.
- Override another player's authority or consent.

The GM can ask, "What does your commander actually know?" when a scene starts
to mix player and character knowledge. A player may always state, "My
commander does not know that," and continue with only the permitted
information.

## Safety and consent expectations

Before play, the GM explains the setting, the fictionalized NATO/Russia
matchup, the communication channels, and the table safety rule. Each
participant may pause for a real-world safety concern. The GM records the
pause and resumes only when everyone agrees.

Players may set boundaries around themes, language, impersonation, violence,
politics, personal experiences, or direct contact scenes. No one has to portray
trauma, humiliation, hatred, abuse, or a personal identity they do not want to
use. A player may fade to black, summarize, redirect, or decline a scene
without losing access to the rules or being penalized for declining it.

The GM should check consent before escalating an interpersonal scene, accept a
simple stop or pause signal without requiring justification, and keep safety
questions out of the game record unless the participant asks for a note. A
pause is not a rules exploit and must not create an in-game advantage or
disadvantage.

## Roleplay and rules

Use this decision test:

1. Is the proposed action within the commander's published authority?
2. Is the relevant information available to that commander?
3. Does the established procedure define how the action resolves?
4. If not, can the GM make a temporary, recorded ruling that is fair and
   symmetrical?

If the answer to the first two questions is yes, roleplay can determine the
commander's intent and presentation. The existing order, timing, information,
movement, contact, combat, and victory procedures still determine the result.
If the action is not legal or not informed, a compelling performance does not
make it legal or informed.

The GM may reward good roleplay with clearer fictional framing, useful
questions, or an NPC response that follows the established situation. The GM
must not grant hidden mechanical advantages, private rerolls, automatic
successes, shorter timers, extra forces, or protection from consequences.

Record a roleplay-driven decision as an ordinary order, report, request, or
ruling. This preserves the authoritative log and makes the effect reviewable
in the playtest report.

## Prototype boundary and future depth

This layer is deliberately smaller than a full character system:

| In this prototype | Future expansion, not required now |
| --- | --- |
| Five-minute commander identity prompts | In-depth character creation and lifepaths |
| One motivation, strength, and complication | Quantified traits, progression, and advancement |
| GM-framed lightweight relationships | A full roster of recurring NPCs and relationship tracks |
| Existing superior/subordinate communication chain | Additional staff/roleplay detail for the already-defined command hierarchy |
| Fictionalized contact within approved procedures | Diplomacy, national politics, and civilian-role systems |
| Roleplay as intent and decision context | Social mechanics, reputation scores, and character abilities |

Do not add future systems during the first test unless the group explicitly
pauses the prototype review and records the change. The purpose of this layer
is to test whether identity, command voice, and relationships make the existing
decisions more meaningful while preserving the smallest playable ruleset.
